## 146.0 (5 issues)

**Question:** Does a cooperative court or forum have jurisdiction under section 91(1) of the Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act, 1960 over a housing society's (or its member's) claim to recover a flat from a licensee or nominal-member occupant, or does the rent legislation (section 28 of the Bombay Rent Act) bar it?

**Rule:** IF `housing_society_possession_dispute and occupant_holds_or_held_under_licence_or_through_member_without_tenancy and dispute_touches_society_business and not landlord_tenant_relationship_protected_by_rent_legislation` THEN `cooperative_forum_has_jurisdiction_and_rent_act_does_not_bar`

**Definition:** A housing society's or member's claim to recover a society flat from a licensee, nominal member or person claiming through a member falls within the cooperative forum's jurisdiction under section 91(1) when it touches the society's business and no protected landlord-tenant relationship exists. Section 28 of the Rent Act applies only to landlord-tenant disputes and does not oust the cooperative forum here.

- `housing_society_possession_dispute`: The proceeding is brought under the cooperative societies legislation by a housing society and/or its member to recover possession of a society flat from an occupant.
- `occupant_holds_or_held_under_licence_or_through_member_without_tenancy`: The occupant was permitted to occupy by the member under a licence (often as a nominal member), or claims through the member, and the occupant is not shown to be a tenant.
- `dispute_touches_society_business`: The dispute concerns the society's actual business as shown by its objects and ordinary activities, for example keeping society flats occupied in accordance with its rules for member housing.
- `landlord_tenant_relationship_protected_by_rent_legislation`: The parties stand in a landlord-tenant relationship, or the occupant is a statutory or deemed tenant, so that the rent legislation governs.
- `cooperative_forum_has_jurisdiction_and_rent_act_does_not_bar`: The cooperative court has jurisdiction under section 91(1) (exclusive in at least one member), and section 28 of the Bombay Rent Act does not exclude it.

**Coherence:** high · covered 5/5 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** All 5 members hold that the cooperative forum has jurisdiction and the Rent Act does not bar the proceeding, because the parties are not landlord and tenant and the dispute touches the society's business.

**Recurring facts:** Housing society, often a tenant co-partnership society, that provides accommodation to its members; Member permitted another person to occupy the flat under a licence, often as a nominal member; Licence terminated or occupation breached society rules, and the occupant stayed on; Occupant asserted tenancy and invoked the exclusive jurisdiction of the Rent Act courts

**Provisions:** Section 91(1) of the Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act, 1960; Section 28 of the Bombay Rents, Hotel and Lodging House Rates (Control) Act, 1947; Section 5(11) of the Bombay Rent Act (definition of tenant); Section 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (res judicata)

**Exceptions/limits:** If the occupant is a tenant or statutory/deemed tenant protected by the rent legislation, the Rent Act forum governs instead (Hindustan Petroleum was distinguished on this ground).; The dispute must touch the society's actual business; Deccan Merchants (a cooperative bank dispute not between the society and a member or person claiming through a member) was distinguished.; Where the society had sold the flat rather than letting it to the member, the dispute may not touch the society's business (Sabharwal Brothers was distinguished).; Jurisdiction is assessed on the claimant's pleadings, and the occupant bears the burden of establishing tenant status.

## 173.0 (5 issues)

**Question:** Can property be requisitioned for a permanent public purpose, and, if so, for how long may a requisition continue before it ceases to be valid because it is indefinite or unreasonably prolonged?

**Rule:** IF `requisition_order_challenged and not requisition_power_confined_to_emergent_needs_by_statute and (not requisition_continued_for_unreasonable_or_indefinite_time) and purpose_is_public` THEN `requisition_is_valid_even_if_purpose_is_permanent`

**Definition:** Under a general power to requisition for a public purpose, the purpose may be permanent, because the permanence of the purpose is distinct from the duration of the requisition. Requisition, however, is inherently temporary, so it ceases to be valid once it continues indefinitely or for an unreasonable period, and the property must then be released or acquired. Emergency statutes that confine requisition to emergent needs cannot be used for permanent purposes.

- `requisition_order_challenged`: The validity or continuation of an order requisitioning property is under challenge.
- `requisition_power_confined_to_emergent_needs_by_statute`: The statute under which the requisition was made is an emergency law whose terms limit requisition to specified emergent purposes and to the period the property is required for them.
- `requisition_continued_for_unreasonable_or_indefinite_time`: The requisition has continued indefinitely or for a period unreasonable in the circumstances of the case, without being replaced by acquisition.
- `purpose_is_public`: The requisition serves a public purpose.
- `requisition_is_valid_even_if_purpose_is_permanent`: The requisition is not invalid merely because the public purpose is permanent in character.

**Coherence:** medium · covered 5/5 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** Roughly 2 of 5 members hold that a permanent public purpose can support requisition under a general public-purpose power (1969775_1, 493891_1). Roughly 2 of 5 hold that requisition cannot continue indefinitely or unreasonably, and the property must be released (1667580_2, 493891_2). One of 5 holds that an emergency-law requisition cannot serve a permanent purpose (286840_2).

**Recurring facts:** Privately owned land or premises were requisitioned by the State for housing, rehabilitation, shops or showrooms.; Requisitions in several cases had continued for decades without acquisition.; Owners argued that a permanent purpose or prolonged continuation made the requisition improper.

**Provisions:** Bombay Land Requisition Act, 1948, ss. 4(5), 5(1), 9; Defence and Internal Security of India Act, 1971, ss. 23(1), 23(3), 30; Entry 42 of List III of the Seventh Schedule; Original Article 31(2) of the Constitution

**Exceptions/limits:** Where the requisitioning statute is an emergency law limited to emergent purposes and to the period of need, a permanent purpose cannot be pursued by requisition (286840_2).; A requisition that is valid when made ceases to be effective after a reasonable period; the property must be released to the owner or acquired on compensation.; What is a reasonable period depends on the circumstances and is ordinarily for the Government to decide. Thirty years was held unreasonable, but it is not a fixed outer limit.; Earlier statements that requisition cannot be made for a permanent purpose were disapproved, while the temporary character of requisition was reaffirmed.; 1667580_2 contains an observation that a permanent purpose from the outset makes requisition improper. This is not followed on that point in 493891_1 and 1969775_1.

**Split suggestion:** Optionally split into (a) permanence of purpose under a general public-purpose requisition power (1969775_1, 493891_1, 286840_2 as a statutory contrast) and (b) the limit on the duration of requisition (1667580_2, 493891_2).

## 20.0 (31 issues)

**Question:** Under what conditions may an appellate court admit additional evidence (Order XLI Rule 27 CPC, Section 391 Cr.P.C., or inherent power), and what must the court do when such an application is made?

**Rule:** IF `appellate_proceeding and additional_evidence_sought and not additional_evidence_merely_to_fill_gaps and ((appellate_court_requires_evidence_to_pronounce_judgment_or_for_substantial_cause and evidence_relevant_and_material) or (evidence_unavailable_despite_due_diligence_or_arose_after_decision and evidence_material_and_justice_requires)) and appellate_court_applies_statutory_conditions_and_records_reasons` THEN `appellate_court_may_admit_additional_evidence_in_its_discretion`

**Definition:** Additional evidence is not receivable in an appeal as of right. An appellate court may admit it, in a judicially exercised and sparing discretion, only on a permitted ground: the court itself needs it to pronounce judgment or for a substantial cause, or it was unavailable despite due diligence or arose after the decision. It must be material, must not be used to fill gaps or order a retrial, and the application must be decided on its merits with reasons recorded.

- `appellate_proceeding`: The matter is an appeal (first, second or Supreme Court, civil or criminal) in which a party applies for, or the court considers, taking additional evidence.
- `additional_evidence_sought`: A party applies, or the appellate court itself proposes, to receive documents or witness testimony not part of the trial record.
- `additional_evidence_merely_to_fill_gaps`: The evidence is sought to patch weaknesses, cure a party's failure to prove its case at trial, or obtain a virtual retrial, although the existing record permits judgment.
- `appellate_court_requires_evidence_to_pronounce_judgment_or_for_substantial_cause`: The appellate court itself finds, after examining the existing record, a lacuna or obscurity such that it needs the evidence to pronounce judgment or for another substantial cause (including risk of failure of justice).
- `evidence_relevant_and_material`: The proposed evidence bears directly and materially on the issues in dispute.
- `evidence_unavailable_despite_due_diligence_or_arose_after_decision`: The party shows that despite due diligence the evidence was not known or could not be produced earlier, or that it came into existence after the decision under appeal.
- `evidence_material_and_justice_requires`: The evidence has a direct and important bearing on the main issue and the interests of justice make its admission necessary.
- `appellate_court_applies_statutory_conditions_and_records_reasons`: The appellate court considers the application on its merits against the prescribed grounds, at the proper stage, and records reasons for admitting the evidence.
- `appellate_court_may_admit_additional_evidence_in_its_discretion`: The appellate court has jurisdiction to admit the additional evidence as a matter of judicial discretion, not of right, and admission does not decide the merits.

**Coherence:** high · covered 24/25 shown · outliers 1

**Typical outcome:** Roughly 13 of 25 members admit or uphold admission, or hold that it may be admitted, where the evidence is needed or unavailable despite diligence or post-dated the decision. About 6 refuse or exclude evidence where the record suffices or it fills gaps. About 5 remit or hold that applications must be decided on merits with reasons.

**Recurring facts:** A party applies in appeal to produce documents (sale deeds, revenue records, wills, public documents) not produced at trial.; The appellate court either found the evidence necessary to pronounce judgment, or found the record sufficient and the evidence an attempt to fill gaps.; Applications were left undecided or decided before the merits hearing without reasons.; Post-decision events (probate, orders, subsequent events) were sought to be placed on record.

**Provisions:** Order XLI Rule 27 CPC; Section 107 CPC; Order XLI Rules 23, 23-A and 25 CPC; Section 391 and Section 386(b) Cr.P.C.; Order 45 Rule 5 Supreme Court Rules (inherent power)

**Exceptions/limits:** Where the existing record suffices for judgment, additional evidence must be refused or ignored.; Evidence cannot be admitted to fill lacunae or patch weak points, or where a party had earlier opportunity and showed no due diligence.; An application must be considered and decided on its merits (generally at the hearing, after examining the record) before the appeal is decided; failure to do so, or admitting evidence without recorded reasons, vitiates reliance on it.; Where additional evidence is admitted and further fact-finding is needed, the court must use remand (O.41 R.23-A) or issue reference (R.25) rather than decide the merits without trial procedure.; Admission of evidence does not amount to acceptance of it on the merits; it remains subject to proof.; In criminal appeals under Section 391 Cr.P.C. the power is broad but sparing, and must not be a disguise for retrial; where exclusion risks failure of justice the evidence must be received.; The Supreme Court may admit post-decision evidence under its inherent power where justice requires.

## 90.0 (9 issues)

**Question:** When does the requirement of a written complaint by a court (Section 195(1)(b)(ii)/(c) CrPC and its predecessor) bar cognizance of forgery and related document offences, and when does it not apply?

**Rule:** IF `document_offence_prosecution and not (offence_committed_after_original_document_produced_or_given_in_evidence_in_court and (offence_committed_by_party_in_that_capacity_where_provision_requires) and original_produced_before_cognizance_taken)` THEN `court_complaint_not_required_and_private_prosecution_or_investigation_may_proceed`

**Definition:** The court-complaint bar on cognizance of document offences applies only where the offence was committed (by a party in that capacity, where the provision so requires) after the original document was produced or given in evidence in court. It does not apply where the forgery preceded production or the party status, where only a copy was produced, or where the original was produced only after cognizance was taken.

- `document_offence_prosecution`: The criminal case concerns forgery, fabrication, or use of a forged document, or related offences, in connection with a document later or earlier relied on in a court or quasi-court proceeding.
- `offence_committed_after_original_document_produced_or_given_in_evidence_in_court`: The alleged offence in respect of the document was committed after the original document itself (not merely a copy) was produced or given in evidence in the court proceeding, i.e. while in the court's custody.
- `offence_committed_by_party_in_that_capacity_where_provision_requires`: Where the clause relied on (Section 195(1)(c)) applies only to offences by a party to the proceeding, the offence was committed by the person after becoming a party and in that capacity.
- `original_produced_before_cognizance_taken`: The original document was already produced in the court proceeding at the time the criminal court took cognizance.
- `court_complaint_not_required_and_private_prosecution_or_investigation_may_proceed`: The bar on cognizance for want of a complaint by the court does not apply, so cognizance, investigation or trial on a private complaint or FIR may proceed.

**Coherence:** high · covered 9/9 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** In all 9 members the bar was held inapplicable and the prosecution or investigation allowed to proceed (about 4 on forgery preceding production, 2 on copy only, 2 on forgery before becoming a party, 1 on original produced after cognizance).

**Recurring facts:** Allegedly forged document (deed, will, receipt, agreement, claim) later relied on in civil, revenue, mutation or probate proceedings; Private complaint or FIR for forgery and related offences; Accused objects that cognizance needs a complaint from the court; Forgery alleged to predate production in court or the accused's becoming a party, or only a copy produced

**Provisions:** Section 195(1)(b)(i) and (ii) CrPC 1973; Section 195(1)(c) CrPC 1973 and 1898 Code; Sections 190, 340-343, 476, 476A, 476B CrPC; Sections 193, 464, 467, 468, 471 IPC

**Exceptions/limits:** The bar applies where the specified offence is committed in respect of the original document after it was produced or given in evidence in court (custodia legis).; Where a single transaction includes an offence needing a court complaint and one that does not, the prosecution cannot be split to evade the bar (approved in one member).; Whether the bar applies to the separate offence of using a forged document as genuine (Section 471) was left open in one member.; Production of the original before cognizance is taken would attract the bar (Gopal Krishna Menon distinguished); production afterwards does not invalidate cognizance already taken.; One member (618763_1) is stated with a rule whose literal 'then' is inverted relative to its conclusion; its conclusion is that the complaint is required only for post-production offences.

## 9.8 (4 issues)

**Question:** Can a conviction for abetment of suicide (Section 306 read with Section 107 IPC) be sustained where the prosecution has not proved instigation or intentional aid by the accused through conduct that is proximate to the suicide, even though a suicide occurred and cruelty or harassment was alleged or partly proved?

**Rule:** IF `appeal_from_suicide_abetment_conviction and suicide_established and not proved_instigating_or_aiding_act_with_intent and not proved_proximate_causal_link_between_accused_conduct_and_suicide` THEN `suicide_abetment_conviction_set_aside`

**Definition:** A Section 306 IPC conviction requires proof of instigation or intentional aid with guilty intent, and a live, proximate link between the accused's conduct and the suicide. Suicide alone, general or remote harassment allegations, speculation, or even proven cruelty under Section 498A, or the discretionary presumption under Section 113-A of the Evidence Act, does not suffice without that proof, and the conviction is then set aside.

- `appeal_from_suicide_abetment_conviction`: The matter is an appeal against a conviction for abetment of suicide (Section 306 IPC), with or without a related cruelty charge.
- `suicide_established`: It is established that the deceased died by suicide.
- `proved_instigating_or_aiding_act_with_intent`: The prosecution proved, beyond reasonable doubt, that the accused instigated the suicide or intentionally aided it by an act or illegal omission, with the necessary guilty intent (Section 107 IPC).
- `proved_proximate_causal_link_between_accused_conduct_and_suicide`: The prosecution proved continuous, live and proximate conduct by the accused (not remote or general allegations) that drove or induced the deceased to commit suicide.
- `suicide_abetment_conviction_set_aside`: The conviction for abetment of suicide cannot be sustained and is set aside, with acquittal of that charge.

**Coherence:** high · covered 4/4 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** All 4 members: the abetment-of-suicide conviction was set aside and the appeal allowed (acquittal in 2 expressly; convictions and sentences set aside in the others).

**Recurring facts:** Deceased died by suicide, usually by consuming poison or pesticide.; Allegations of harassment or cruelty, often related to property or dowry demands, were general, remote in time, or unproved.; No specific positive act or incitement by the accused proximate to the suicide was shown.; Courts below inferred abetment from the circumstances or from the fact of suicide.; Prosecution evidence was hearsay, contradictory, or contradicted by documents or letters.

**Provisions:** Sections 306 and 107 IPC; Section 498A IPC (cruelty) with its Explanation; Section 113-A of the Indian Evidence Act; Section 34 IPC

**Exceptions/limits:** Where the prosecution proves the statutory preconditions (suicide within seven years of marriage and cruelty), the court may presume abetment under Section 113-A of the Evidence Act. The presumption is discretionary, not automatic, and must be weighed against all circumstances, including a cause-and-effect link between cruelty and suicide.; Proof of cruelty under Section 498A IPC is a distinct offence and does not by itself establish abetment of suicide.; The rule is about failure of proof; where an instigating act or intentional aid with proximate causal connection is proved, a conviction may stand.; In one member, the absence of any charge alleging abetment or driving to suicide was an additional ground for setting aside the conviction.

## 255.0 (4 issues)

**Question:** Where a lease contains an option to renew, what is the legal effect and scope of that option: does exercising it alone renew the lease, does it carry into a later or fresh lease that incorporates the original terms, and is it enforceable despite a fixed term or objections to perpetual renewal?

**Rule:** IF `lease_contains_renewal_option and (original_lease_terms_incorporated_into_later_lease or renewal_covenant_creates_no_property_interest or lessee_exercised_option) and not (renewal_claim_denied and no_renewal_instrument)` THEN `renewal_option_is_enforceable_as_a_term_of_the_lease_but_renewal_requires_a_fresh_instrument`

**Definition:** A renewal option is a personal covenant that survives into a later lease which incorporates the original terms, is independent of the fixed initial term, and is not void as perpetuity. Exercising it does not by itself renew the lease, because a fresh lease instrument is needed, and a renewed lease need not itself contain a further renewal option.

- `lease_contains_renewal_option`: The original lease or agreement gives the lessee an option to renew for a further term.
- `original_lease_terms_incorporated_into_later_lease`: A renewed or fresh lease (by deed or compromise) incorporates the covenants or terms of the original lease for matters not otherwise addressed, without reserving the renewal option.
- `renewal_covenant_creates_no_property_interest`: The renewal covenant is a personal covenant and does not itself transfer property or create an interest in property beyond the initial term.
- `lessee_exercised_option`: The lessee has validly exercised the renewal option.
- `renewal_claim_denied`: The other party expressly denies that the lease has been renewed.
- `no_renewal_instrument`: No fresh lease deed or document evidencing renewal has been executed and no court declaration of renewal exists.
- `renewal_option_is_enforceable_as_a_term_of_the_lease_but_renewal_requires_a_fresh_instrument`: The renewal option stays a valid and enforceable term, which can be enforced by obtaining a fresh lease instrument, but exercising it alone does not create the renewed lease.

**Coherence:** medium · covered 4/4 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** The option is generally upheld as an enforceable term (3 of 4: perpetuity challenge rejected, option carried into renewed lease, option survives fixed term), while one member holds that exercising the option does not by itself renew the lease without a fresh instrument.

**Recurring facts:** The original lease or agreement contained an option to renew.; A dispute arose when the lessee sought or exercised renewal and the other party refused or denied it.; A later deed or compromise incorporated or referred to the original lease terms.

**Provisions:** Transfer of Property Act, 1882, sections 5, 14, 40 and 105

**Exceptions/limits:** Exercise of the option alone does not create a renewed lease where the other party denies renewal and no renewal instrument or court declaration exists.; Courts lean against perpetual renewal and require clear and unambiguous language for successive or perpetual renewals.; Whether a renewal covenant carries into a renewed lease depends on the language of the covenants, the renewed deed, the parties' intention and the circumstances read as a whole.; The renewed lease granted on enforcement need not itself include a further renewal covenant.

**Split suggestion:** If a tighter grouping is wanted, separate (a) whether exercise of the option itself creates a renewal (1399941_2), (b) validity of the option against the rule against perpetuity (706590_1), and (c) carry-over of the option into renewed or fresh leases through incorporation of terms (745068_1, 846382_3).

## 150.0 (5 issues)

**Question:** Is property owned by a State Government in a Union Territory exempt from property tax (including municipal property tax) imposed under parliamentary authority, as exempt from Union taxation under Article 289(1) of the Constitution?

**Rule:** IF `state_owned_property_in_union_territory and property_tax_imposed_under_law_with_parliamentary_force and not property_used_for_state_trade_or_business` THEN `state_property_exempt_from_property_tax_as_union_taxation`

**Definition:** Property taxes, including municipal property taxes, imposed in a Union Territory under laws having parliamentary force are Union taxation, so State Government property there is exempt under Article 289(1). The exemption does not extend to property used for a State trade or business, subject to Article 289(3).

- `state_owned_property_in_union_territory`: The property is owned by a State Government and is situated in a Union Territory.
- `property_tax_imposed_under_law_with_parliamentary_force`: The property tax is imposed under a law made by Parliament for the Union Territory, or a law (including a municipal enactment) that has force there through parliamentary enactment or extension, so that the municipal body's taxing power derives from Parliament.
- `property_used_for_state_trade_or_business`: The property is used or occupied for a trade or business carried on by or on behalf of the State Government, and not declared by Parliament to be incidental to ordinary governmental functions.
- `state_property_exempt_from_property_tax_as_union_taxation`: The property tax is Union taxation within Article 289(1), and the State property is exempt from it.

**Coherence:** medium · covered 4/5 shown · outliers 1

**Typical outcome:** In all 4 covered members, the tax is held to be Union taxation and State property is exempt, with the trade or business qualification noted in 1. The outlier (1) holds Union property exempt from municipal tax under Article 285.

**Recurring facts:** Municipal bodies assessed property tax on land and buildings owned or occupied by State Governments in a Union Territory.; The municipal laws were enacted by Parliament or extended to the territory by parliamentary legislation.; The States claimed the constitutional exemption from Union taxation.

**Provisions:** Article 289(1), (2) and (3); Article 246(4); Articles 265, 243X and 243ZB; Article 285 (outlier); Section 155 of the Government of India Act, 1935

**Exceptions/limits:** Article 289(2): the exemption does not apply to property used or occupied for a trade or business carried on by or on behalf of the State, unless Parliament has declared that trade or business incidental to ordinary governmental functions under Article 289(3).; The rule concerns State-owned property under Article 289(1); Union-owned property is governed by Article 285 (see the outlier).

**Split suggestion:** Keep the four Article 289(1) members on State property in Union Territories together. Move 164765023_1 (Article 285, Union property and the pre-Constitution saving) to a separate cluster on Union property immunity from local taxes.

## 98.0 (8 issues)

**Question:** In environmental public-interest or writ proceedings, is a person who causes pollution or unauthorised ecological damage liable to pay compensation for the damage, including the cost of restoring the environment, and what restorative or monetary relief may the court order?

**Rule:** IF `(environmental_public_interest_proceeding and responsible_party_caused_pollution_or_ecological_damage and environmental_or_personal_harm_resulted)` THEN `responsible_party_must_bear_cost_of_compensation_and_environmental_restoration`

**Definition:** Under the polluter-pays principle, a party whose activity pollutes or degrades the environment is liable to compensate those harmed and to bear the cost of restoring the environment. The court may direct assessment of the amount, order restoration with enforcement at the party's cost, and in Article 32 proceedings may add exemplary damages.

- `environmental_public_interest_proceeding`: The matter is a public-interest, writ or Article 32 proceeding concerning environmental harm.
- `responsible_party_caused_pollution_or_ecological_damage`: The party carried on an activity (for example polluting industry, unauthorised works on a watercourse, or unlawful alteration of a structure) that polluted or degraded the environment or ecology.
- `environmental_or_personal_harm_resulted`: The activity caused damage to the environment or ecology, or loss or hardship to affected people.
- `responsible_party_must_bear_cost_of_compensation_and_environmental_restoration`: The responsible party is liable to compensate affected persons where they suffered loss and to pay for, or itself carry out, restoration of the damaged environment (the court may also award exemplary damages as a deterrent).

**Coherence:** medium · covered 6/8 shown · outliers 2

**Typical outcome:** Of the 8 members, 6 hold the responsible party liable to compensate and/or restore the environment, with quantum to be assessed (one adds exemplary damages and one orders restoration at the party's cost); 2 find disobedience of court orders in contempt context.

**Recurring facts:** Environmental degradation from industrial effluent, aquaculture, or unauthorised works on a river or coastal bund; Proceedings brought as public-interest, Article 32 or contempt matters; Expert reports or inspections documenting harm; Court directs assessment of compensation or restoration cost by an authority or expert

**Provisions:** Article 21, Articles 47, 48A and 51A(g) of the Constitution; Article 32 (power to award damages); Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) 5 SCC 647; Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India (1996) 3 SCC 212; Polluter-pays, precautionary and absolute liability principles

**Exceptions/limits:** The amount of compensation and restoration cost is left to expert or authority assessment, not fixed by the principle.; Exemplary damages are a separate civil remedy with different considerations from a criminal fine and were only put to show-cause in the member that considered them.; Directions to restore the prior condition at the party's cost were made in a contempt context after deliberate disobedience and where restoration was feasible.; Two members (contempt findings of disobedience of restraint or permission conditions) rest on disobedience of court orders rather than on polluter-pays liability.; Absolute liability was stated for hazardous or inherently dangerous activities.

**Split suggestion:** Separate the contempt-disobedience members (1520994_3, 17698471_1, and arguably 1520994_4) from the polluter-pays compensation/restoration members (1514672_2, 1934103_3, 507684_5, 98864672_3). Treat exemplary damages (98864672_4) as a sub-issue of the polluter-pays group.

## 232.0 (4 issues)

**Question:** Where a decree requires the plaintiff to deposit a sum (such as the pre-emption price) by a fixed time, does failure to deposit by that deadline cause the suit to stand dismissed, regardless of a pending appeal or the absence of an express dismissal direction in the decree?

**Rule:** IF `decree_requires_deposit_by_deadline and required_deposit_not_made_by_deadline_in_required_manner and not decree_suspended_or_deadline_extended_by_competent_court` THEN `suit_stands_dismissed`

**Definition:** Where a decree fixes a time (and, if specified, a mode) for the plaintiff's deposit, non-compliance causes the suit to stand dismissed automatically. In pre-emption cases this follows from the mandatory Order XX Rule 14 CPC, even if the decree omits an express dismissal direction, and filing an appeal or later altering the decree does not suspend the obligation or extend the time without a stay or an express extension.

- `decree_requires_deposit_by_deadline`: The decree (in a pre-emption suit under Order XX Rule 14 CPC, or a peremptory decree that makes non-compliance self-executing) requires the plaintiff or buyer to deposit a sum within a fixed time.
- `required_deposit_not_made_by_deadline_in_required_manner`: The plaintiff or buyer did not deposit the required sum within the time fixed by the decree, or did not deposit it in the manner the decree prescribed.
- `decree_suspended_or_deadline_extended_by_competent_court`: An appellate court stayed the decree or lawfully altered it, relieving the plaintiff of the deposit obligation or extending the deadline; merely filing an appeal, withdrawing it, or altering the decree in other respects does not count.
- `suit_stands_dismissed`: The suit stands dismissed by operation of law or of the decree, and the decree cannot be executed.

**Coherence:** high · covered 4/4 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** All 4 members hold that the suit stands dismissed for non-deposit by the deadline (3 under Order XX Rule 14 in pre-emption, 1 under a peremptory specific-performance decree).

**Recurring facts:** A decree fixed a deadline for depositing money (price or balance consideration).; The plaintiff or buyer failed to deposit by the deadline.; An appeal was filed or the appellate court intervened, but there was no stay or effective alteration of the payment direction.; The decree sometimes omitted an express dismissal direction.

**Provisions:** Order XX Rule 14, Code of Civil Procedure; Naguba Appa v. Namdev, AIR 1954 SC 50; Dattaraya v. Shaikh Mahboob Shaikh Ali, [1969] 2 SCR 514

**Exceptions/limits:** A stay granted by the appellate court may relieve the pre-emptor of the deposit obligation while it operates, because the decree imposes reciprocal obligations.; If the appellate court validly alters the decree or fixes a new time, the new terms govern.; An appellate court cannot extend the deadline merely because it alters the decree in other respects.; The specific-performance member rests on the peremptory terms of the decree rather than a statutory provision, so automatic dismissal there depends on the decree itself making non-compliance self-executing.

## 137.0 (6 issues)

**Question:** Is a lease granted during the subsistence of a mortgage, whether by the mortgagor in possession or by the mortgagee in possession, binding on the other party to the mortgage, on the redeeming owner, or on an auction-purchaser, and does it bind only if made in the ordinary or prudent course of management, as strictly proved by the party relying on it?

**Provisions:** Section 34 IPC; Section 149 IPC; Sections 141, 147 and 148 IPC; Sections 300 and 302 IPC; Sections 304 Part I and Part II IPC; Sections 325 and 326 IPC; Sections 96 to 106 IPC (private defence)

**Exceptions/limits:** Liability remains where the evidence proves prior concert or a common intention formed during the occurrence, with participation in furtherance of it.; An accused who personally inflicted an injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death can be liable under Section 302 without Section 149.; Absence of an overt act does not by itself exclude Section 149 liability if the offence was within the common object or known to be likely.; Where the common object or intention covers a lesser offence (grievous hurt, hurt with dangerous weapons, injuries likely to cause death), the accused remain liable for that offence, constructively where applicable.; A person who exceeds the right of private defence may be individually liable if the evidence establishes his own responsibility.; Where the appellate court has not displaced a finding of no common intention, the mere fact of a joint charge cannot support conviction.

## 340.0 (3 issues)

**Question:** When a firearm is discharged at or near a person, does the act amount to attempt to murder under Section 307 IPC, which turns on whether the accused fired with intent to kill or knowledge that death could result, even if the shot misses or death is not proved to be caused?

**Rule:** IF `firearm_discharged_at_victim and intent_to_kill_or_knowledge_of_likely_death_proved and not (hit_on_another_person_was_merely_accidental_result_of_single_shot_without_such_intent)` THEN `attempted_murder_conviction_under_section_307_sustained`

**Definition:** Firing a gun at a person with proved intent to kill or knowledge that death could result supports an attempt-to-murder conviction under Section 307 IPC, even if the shots miss or the causal link to death is unproved. It does not when the only harm was an accidental pellet injury to another person from a single shot.

- `firearm_discharged_at_victim`: The accused aimed and fired a firearm at the victim, whether or not the shot hit or caused the victim's death.
- `intent_to_kill_or_knowledge_of_likely_death_proved`: The evidence establishes that the accused fired with intent to kill, or with knowledge that the act could cause death.
- `hit_on_another_person_was_merely_accidental_result_of_single_shot_without_such_intent`: The evidence shows only one shot was fired and another person was hit accidentally by pellets from it, with no proved intent or knowledge directed at that person.
- `attempted_murder_conviction_under_section_307_sustained`: The conviction for attempt to murder under Section 307 IPC is upheld, or substituted for a murder conviction that fails for want of proved causation of death.

**Coherence:** medium · covered 3/3 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** Two of three members sustain or substitute an attempted-murder conviction (a missed shot; a shooting without proved causation of death). One restores an acquittal where a single shot accidentally hit a bystander.

**Recurring facts:** Firearm discharged at or near a person; Outcome under Section 307 depends on findings about intent or knowledge and on the evidence of how the injury occurred; Appeals reviewing findings of fact on shots, injuries and medical evidence

**Provisions:** Section 307 IPC; Section 299 IPC; Section 302 IPC (murder conviction replaced)

**Exceptions/limits:** Where evidence shows only one shot and an accidental pellet injury to a bystander, an attempted-murder conviction is not sustained and an acquittal on that charge stands.; Substitution of attempted murder for murder requires that the shooting and the intent or knowledge be proved, even though the causal link to death is not.; Members cite no authority for the missed-shot point; the substitution point relies on a precedent about shooting without proof that the shot caused death.

**Split suggestion:** Could be split into (a) shooting at a victim with intent or knowledge supports Section 307 even when the shot misses or death is unproved (1328394_4, 47172852_1), and (b) accidental pellet injury from a single shot does not support Section 307 (1620689_2).

## 37.1 (9 issues)

**Question:** Can a High Court, in exercising inherent (or revisional) jurisdiction before trial, quash criminal proceedings or charges by assessing disputed facts or evidence, where the allegations disclose an offence and the evidence needs testing at trial?

**Rule:** IF `criminal_proceeding_challenged_for_quashing_or_charge_quashed and allegations_or_prosecution_material_disclose_offence_prima_facie and quashing_depends_on_assessing_disputed_facts_or_evidence_reliability and not legal_bar_or_facially_non_offence` THEN `quashing_on_evidence_assessment_is_improper_and_proceedings_continue`

**Definition:** Where the allegations or prosecution material prima facie disclose an offence, a court exercising inherent or revisional power must not quash proceedings or charges by appreciating disputed evidence or deciding triable factual questions. Those questions are left to the trial court, and quashing is reserved for cases of legal bar, no offence on the face of the allegations, or manifest absence of evidence.

- `criminal_proceeding_challenged_for_quashing_or_charge_quashed`: A party seeks quashing of a criminal complaint, FIR, investigation, or framed charge, or a court below has quashed it, under inherent or revisional jurisdiction.
- `allegations_or_prosecution_material_disclose_offence_prima_facie`: Taken at face value, the allegations and the material collected (FIR, charge sheet, or other prosecution material) disclose the alleged offence or create strong suspicion of it.
- `quashing_depends_on_assessing_disputed_facts_or_evidence_reliability`: Granting quashing would require the court to resolve factual disputes, weigh documents, witness contradictions or untested material, or judge the reliability or sufficiency of evidence as a trial court would.
- `legal_bar_or_facially_non_offence`: A legal bar to the proceedings exists, or the allegations accepted in full do not constitute the offence, or there is no legal evidence or the evidence is manifestly inconsistent with the accusation.
- `quashing_on_evidence_assessment_is_improper_and_proceedings_continue`: Quashing on that basis is not justified, the order quashing is set aside or refused, and the matter proceeds to investigation or trial with defences left open.

**Coherence:** medium · covered 8/9 shown · outliers 1

**Typical outcome:** In about 8 of 9 members the quashing order was set aside or refused, or the appeal against refusal to quash was dismissed, and the proceedings continued. The remaining member (157386110_1) set aside the High Court's procedural overreach.

**Recurring facts:** The High Court quashed or interfered with proceedings after examining evidence, documents, or witness statements that had not been tested at trial.; The allegations or charge sheet disclosed offences such as fraud, forgery, or misrepresentation.; Factual disputes (genuineness of documents, contradictions, classification of a mineral) needed evidence.; The Supreme Court was often hearing an appeal against a quashing order.

**Provisions:** Section 482 Cr.P.C. 1973; Section 561-A Cr.P.C. 1898; Section 397 Cr.P.C. (revision); Sections 154, 156, 173(2) Cr.P.C.; Article 136 of the Constitution

**Exceptions/limits:** Quashing remains available where a legal bar applies or the allegations, taken at face value, do not disclose the offence (Bhajan Lal / R. P. Kapur categories).; Inherent power may be used where there is no legal evidence or the evidence is clearly or manifestly inconsistent with the accusation.; After a charge sheet is filed, the court must consider the material collected in investigation, but still cannot appreciate disputed evidence.; Where investigation is incomplete, intervention is generally premature.; Procedural limit: a High Court cannot bar submission of the police report to the Magistrate or rely on an unsubmitted draft charge sheet to quash.

**Split suggestion:** Keep the eight members on the bar against assessing disputed evidence when quashing as the core group. Treat 157386110_1 (restriction on filing the police report and reliance on a draft charge sheet) as a separate procedural-overreach cluster. 1241270_1 could also be moved to a group on non-interference at the interlocutory stage.

## 297.0 (3 issues)

**Question:** Will the Supreme Court reconsider or depart from an earlier decision that governs the issue when no compelling, substantial or special reason for reopening it has been shown?

**Rule:** IF `request_to_reconsider_earlier_supreme_court_decision and earlier_decision_governs_the_issue and not compelling_or_substantial_reason_to_reopen` THEN `earlier_decision_is_not_reconsidered`

**Definition:** The Supreme Court will not reopen an earlier decision that governs the issue unless the party shows compelling, substantial or extraordinary reasons. A merely preferable alternative view, or a repeat of grounds already rejected, is not enough, particularly where the decision has been long applied or followed or disturbing it would unsettle other decisions.

- `request_to_reconsider_earlier_supreme_court_decision`: A party asks the Supreme Court to reconsider, revise or refer to a larger bench an earlier Supreme Court decision.
- `earlier_decision_governs_the_issue`: The earlier decision directly decides or governs the question now raised, and has been applied, followed or relied upon (for example, long applied, followed in later cases, or recent).
- `compelling_or_substantial_reason_to_reopen`: The party has shown compelling, substantial, special or extraordinary grounds for reopening the earlier decision, such as a serious infirmity, a public-interest need, or a valid and compelling reason. It is not enough that another interpretation is preferable or that grounds already rejected are repeated.
- `earlier_decision_is_not_reconsidered`: The Court declines to reconsider or refer the earlier decision and applies it.

**Coherence:** high · covered 3/3 shown · outliers 0

**Typical outcome:** All 3 members decline to reconsider the earlier decision and apply it.

**Recurring facts:** A party asks the Court to reconsider or refer an earlier decision that directly governs the issue.; The earlier decision has been long applied, followed in later cases, or is a recent Constitution Bench ruling.; No compelling or special ground beyond a preferable alternative view or repeated arguments is shown.

**Provisions:** Keshav Mills Co. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax (test for revising earlier decisions); Sri Sankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India (constitutional amendment challenge); Md. Serajuddin v. State of Orissa (stare decisis)

**Exceptions/limits:** Reconsideration may be undertaken where compelling and substantial reasons make revision necessary in the public interest or for another valid and compelling reason.; The Court weighs the alleged infirmity in the earlier decision, its effect on the public good, how compelling the contrary arguments are, and the consequences of disturbing settled decisions.; For recent Constitution Bench decisions, only extraordinary or special reasons justify reconsideration.
